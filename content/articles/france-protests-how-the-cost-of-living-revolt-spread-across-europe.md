## What started the protests
 
The France protests began in mid-September, when high school students walked out over overcrowded classes, teacher shortages and decaying buildings. The movement soon took in fuel prices, wages and social housing, and trade unions joined.
 
On 17 October, [more than 500,000 people protested nationwide](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/8/mapping-how-frances-protest-wave-spread-across-europe), according to government estimates. A union-organised rally days before 7 November drew an estimated 575,000. On 7 November, the Interior Ministry counted 146,000 demonstrators nationwide, 46,000 of them in Paris. Unions gave much higher figures of 250,000 and 75,000. Turnout has fallen from earlier weeks.
 
Peaceful marches have at times escalated into arson, damage to public property and clashes with police. Data from the Armed Conflict Location & Event Data project (ACLED) shows how unusual this is. It logged more than 1,200 protests and riots of at least 100 people in France between 15 September and 30 October.
 
ACLED chief executive [Clionadh Raleigh said](https://acleddata.com/expert-comment/france-taking-streets-why) daily protest events had run at roughly three times the five-year average. Almost a quarter of events were classed as riots, against a typical figure under 2 percent.
 
About 70,000 security personnel were deployed around the 7 November protests. Police used tear gas and batons in Paris and fired tear gas at people blocking a road in Lyon.
 
On 28 October, [four UN human rights experts](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/28/un-experts-sound-alarm-at-excessive-use-of-force-in-french-student-protests) warned of excessive force against student demonstrators, saying the arrest and detention of several thousand people, mostly minors, looked disproportionate. In Lille, a teenager lost a hand when a grenade exploded during clashes between school protesters and police.
 
## How it spread
 
ACLED data shows more than 1,700 comparable protests and riots across 31 European countries in the same period. Spain recorded 173, Italy 160, Germany 70 and Portugal 33.
 
In Germany, hooded rioters [looted stores](https://www.reuters.com/world/europe/gen-z-protests-reach-germany-over-160000-expected-halloween-rallies-2026-10-31/) after tens of thousands took to the streets in Frankfurt to demand better education and lower living costs. Italy saw [clashes](https://www.reuters.com/world/italys-meloni-boost-police-arrest-powers-response-weekend-riots-2026-10-20/) between police and protesters over poor public services and the rising cost of living.
 
In Spain, tens of thousands rallied after parliament rejected two emergency housing decrees on 2 October, a setback for Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez's minority government. Police have since [cleared the remaining housing protesters](https://www.reuters.com/world/spanish-police-clear-remaining-housing-protesters-madrid-encampment-2026-11-03/) from a Madrid encampment.
 
Portugal has had large marches over housing and wages, and police [cleared protest camps](https://www.reuters.com/world/portuguese-police-clear-housing-protest-camps-arresting-55-2026-10-20/), arresting 55.
 
[Alberto Alemanno of HEC Paris](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/17/cost-of-living-protests-hit-belgium-italy-portugal-and-france) called France the "epicentre" of a movement that is now spreading.
 
## What comes next
 
ACLED's Europe analyst Joel Crisetig [warned](https://acleddata.com/expert-comment/what-will-it-take-end-frances-relentless-protests) that France's worsening finances and the rejection of attempts to pass a 2027 budget leave the authorities with few options. Without far-reaching reforms, he said, demonstrations could continue and may turn violent.
 
Renaud Foucart of Lancaster University [expects the protests to fade](https://www.france24.com/en/tv-shows/perspective/20261102-no-political-solution-to-the-anger-in-france-expert-says-amid-ongoing-protests), noting that most high schools have reopened and weekend turnout has fallen three weeks running. But he said there is no political solution, because the government lacks a parliamentary majority.
